About

Small-boat harbours of the Oslofjord

This map shows the physical outline of every small-boat harbour found so far in the municipalities around the inner and outer Oslofjord: piers, berths and the water between them. 760 polygons from 22 municipalities are mapped; the municipalities marked not yet mapped are next.

Flyover route

Start in Oslo, head south-west down the fjord, then cross over and fly north again.

What counts as a harbour

A shared facility with at least 10 berths, or a documented marina or boat club with permanent berths. Scattered private piers, bathing piers, ferry quays and planned facilities are left out.

How it was made

  1. Collect candidates from two independent sources: OpenStreetMap piers and marinas, and public registers, place names and municipal harbour pages.
  2. Draft outlines automatically: nearby piers are clustered, buffered and clipped to the water area of Kartverket’s topographic map and to the municipal boundary.
  3. Review every candidate against georeferenced aerial imagery. Each one is included, trimmed, split, redrawn by hand or excluded, and the reason is logged.
  4. Sweep all imagery tiles along the shore to catch harbours that are missing from OpenStreetMap, and draw them by hand.
  5. Quality-check and approve each municipality: overlays on the imagery, geometry and boundary checks, and a manual approval before anything enters the combined dataset.

Limitations

Image acquisition dates are unknown, no field survey has been made, and small facilities may be missed. Outlines are mapping estimates, not official harbour or property boundaries. Uncertain outlines are flagged in each harbour card.

Methodology

How the harbours were mapped

An AI agent mapped the harbours one municipality at a time, following a written methodology. A person approved every municipality by inspecting its GeoJSON before it was added to the dataset.

Data sources

Esri World ImageryAerial and satellite imagery, exported as georeferenced tiles at about 0.67–0.83 m/pixel in EPSG:25833. This is the main evidence for every outline. Acquisition dates are unknown.
OpenStreetMap (Overpass API)Piers, breakwaters, marinas and moorings. Used to find candidates and as the skeleton of draft outlines. © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL.
KartverketMunicipal boundaries (Kommuneinfo API), the topographic map (WMS) for shoreline, water area and place names, and the place-name register. The sea chart is used only in this viewer.
Brønnøysund Register CentreThe Central Coordinating Register for Legal Entities (Enhetsregisteret): boat clubs, marinas and pier associations. A club’s address is never treated as the harbour’s location.
Public documentsMunicipal harbour pages, operator websites, waste-management plans for harbours and planning documents. Used for operation and published berth counts.

Analysis, step by step

  1. Coverage. The municipal boundary is converted to EPSG:25833 and the shoreline is split into cells. Overview and 1.1 × 1.1 km detail tiles are stored with their bounding box, retrieval date and SHA-256 checksum.
  2. Candidates. OpenStreetMap is queried in 3 × 3 sub-areas. The register and place names are searched separately, so the two sources can check each other.
  3. Draft outlines (method 1.2). Piers within 60 m of each other are clustered, buffered by 12 m and closed by 20 m. The result is clipped to the map’s water area (+4 m) and to the municipal boundary.
  4. Visual review. Review sheets show the draft (red), OSM piers (yellow) and the boundary on the imagery. Each candidate is included, trimmed, split, extended, redrawn by hand or excluded, and the reason is written down. Classification follows the ten-berth rule.
  5. Gap sweep. Contact sheets of all shoreline tiles are checked for harbours missing from OpenStreetMap. These are drawn by hand, in metres.
  6. Polygon rule. An outline covers the piers, berths and water between them. It does not cover the bay, land or club property. Harbours that cross a municipal boundary are clipped and linked across the boundary.

The method changed over time. Holmestrand and Horten are early pilots traced from the topographic map. Tønsberg to Råde were drawn by hand on aerial imagery (method 1.0–1.1). From Fredrikstad onwards, OSM-assisted drafts were reviewed against imagery (method 1.2).

AI models and tools

AI models: the mapping was done with a mix of Anthropic Claude Opus 5.5 and OpenAI GPT-6.1 Sol. Both models followed the same methodology and the same instructions.

What the AI did: it wrote and ran the processing scripts, retrieved the sources, looked at imagery tiles and review sheets with its built-in image understanding to classify candidates and adjust outlines, and logged every decision.

What it did not do: no object-detection or segmentation model was trained or used, and no harbour was classified automatically from pixels. All geometry operations are ordinary, repeatable code (Python with Shapely, PyProj and Pillow).

Human role: a person set the rules, approved every municipality and pointed out harbours that had been missed. Those corrections led to method changes, such as cross-checking against OpenStreetMap piers.

Quality control

After the last edit, every exported polygon is checked as an overlay on the imagery. The automated checks are: valid geometry, unique IDs, 0 m² outside the municipal boundary, no overlap within a municipality, with neighbours or with the combined file, and CSV matching GeoJSON. Uncertain outlines and open candidates are kept and flagged, not removed.
